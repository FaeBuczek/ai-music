Ethical Concerns

There is no consistent standard for transparency or disclosure when it comes to AI-generated music. A listener may consume AI music without ever knowing it, raising questions about informed consent in the media we engage with daily. The use of real artists' work to train AI models without their knowledge or compensation is another pressing issue, as it constitutes a form of intellectual labor theft that current copyright law is not equipped to address. There is also the question of cultural authenticity. Music has historically been a deeply human expression of lived experience, and the commodification of that expression through algorithmic generation challenges what we consider art to be. The economic displacement of session musicians, producers, and songwriters who depend on their craft for income is a tangible harm that is already beginning to materialize. Finally, the concentration of generative AI tools in the hands of well-resourced companies risks creating new gatekeepers in an industry that has only recently begun democratizing through independent distribution, meaning the power dynamics shift.

What Do Artists Have To Say?

Catherine Anne Davies, who records as the Anchoress and also sits on the board of directors at the Featured Artists Coalition (FAC), has many reservations here. "Most people don't even want their work to be used for training AI," she says. "I'm on the dystopian side, or maybe what I call the realist side of things. I'm interested in the way that AI can be assistive in the creative process – if it can make us more efficient, if it can streamline our processes. But generative AI for me, in terms of creative output, is a big no-no at the moment. I'm yet to be convinced."

What Do Pro-AI Individuals Have To Say?

Paul Sinclair, CMO of Suno — one of the biggest AI-music producing software — has claimed that his company's platform is about "empowerment" that enables "billions of fans to create and play with music." Sinclair has argued that the present copyright laws are "walled gardens" that deny people access.

There Are No Clear Guidelines

Those in support of generative AI music also hold a valid ethical position that deserves equal consideration in this discourse. Artists and creators who choose to incorporate AI into their process have a fundamental right to experiment with and adopt emerging technology, just as musicians throughout history have embraced synthesizers, drum machines, and digital audio workstations, all of which faced similar criticism in their time. The freedom to create without prescriptive limitations on the tools one uses is itself an ethical stance rooted in artistic autonomy. Additionally, generative AI has the potential to democratize music production by lowering the barrier to entry for creators who lack access to expensive equipment, formal training, or industry connections. For some, AI is not a replacement for human creativity but an extension of it. The interests of this group, including their freedom of expression, access to innovation, and right to participate in and shape an evolving industry, are just as deserving of protection and consideration as those of the artists who choose not to use it.
