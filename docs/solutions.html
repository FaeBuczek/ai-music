Solutions

Some different options we brainstormed are as follows. Allow AI use, Add disclaimers, Ban ai use. We want to understand the implications of different copyrights, the legal infrastructure surrounding AI music, and potential different pay for those who use AI for their music.

Allow AI use laissez-faire style

(Utilitarian, increases overall benefit/advancement)

This bolsters the rights to artists to explore the creative process either with or without AI use and to any degree. With this option this would mean adding onto the possibilities at the disposal of artists to create their projects. At the same time, this does pose threats to artists’ rights to their own intellectual property, as pursuing this option risks turning copyrighted music into training data to be used in the creation of future music. This option doesn’t fairly compensate artists whose work is being used for this purpose. However, it is important to ask if the current system (where all music is inspired by what the creator listens to in some way) is any more fair or just? It is not currently the expectation that artists compensate those that they are influenced by.

The idea of a laissez-faire approach to GenAI use in music does support a more communal vision for the development of music. This would arguably maximise profits for music streaming services like Spotify. It would add a collaborative element to the development of music in general. It would make music creation more accessible to those who might be stronger at making music with AI assistance and who do not feel confident making their own music. It could, however, disadvantage artists who choose to not use it, which is not a perfectly balanced communal approach.

Additionally, in allowing free use of GenAI in the creation of music, we would have to abandon the virtue of encouraging originality as well as that of rewarding creative accomplishments. The art that people invest their lives into creating is something that is worth preserving, protecting, and importantly, rewarding.

Add disclaimers

(Common Good, supports everyone)

While benefiting from the advantages to artists listed above, adding disclaimers also can respect the rights of the listeners who may feel strongly about GenAI music to not support it. Providing a disclaimer that the music uses GenAI influence rightly gives the listener the choice to listen or not with full understanding of the situation. This also begins to give some level of recognition for the artists in the training data, while not specifically recognizing individual artists.

Similarly, from a communal perspective, this approach serves all of the stakeholders in the ways listed above while also giving more information to the consumer, who may decide for themselves if they would prefer to listen to GenAI music or not. This could also work to counterbalance the skew towards GenAI artists that we mention as a concern in the laissez-faire approach.

This approach adds an extra layer of virtue into the conversation: transparency. Being up front about whether or not AI is being used in the process, as AI gets stronger and stronger and thus better at mimicking non-AI material, it has quickly become an issue of honesty. In other applications of AI (such as a food and recipe Youtube channel that is AI generated), it is seen as dishonest and manipulative for people who may enjoy the content without knowing for certain that it is AI. For the sake of such people, this added transparency is virtuous.

Ban AI use (in monetized settings)

(Fairness/Justice, as we do not exploit creators/OG musicians)

This is the ultimate way to protect the rights of individual artists and the work they create. If we decide that GenAI cannot be used to create music that will be profited off of, people will still have the ability to use GenAI for personal projects. The only difference would be that those projects could not then be used for profit on Spotify, youtube, etc., further protecting the rights of artists.

Adopting the no-AI approach would serve the majority of stakeholders while really only leaving behind the AI-using artists. In the end of the day, music streaming would not be significantly harmed by this ban, seeing as they have always historically been provided with enough new musical content to thrive, artists who don’t use AI will be able to continue “business as usual” in their creative process. In this model, the conscientious objector to AI music’s desires would be prioritized over those of the artist who prefers to use AI.

The rejection of AI in any endeavor dealing with financial gain boils down to a respect for the creators and their fans. Similarly, streaming platforms should represent their artists and uplift art as a whole as an inherent ethical dilemma of their existence. Ultimately, since they profit off of the art, they must work to uplift and support their music, which the rejection of AI serves to do.
